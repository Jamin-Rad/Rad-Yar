## Treatment kept separate from classification

The PDF page is not a treatment source. The linked Mason II treatment summary now says that the page's Type II thresholds classify morphology and do not alone justify ORIF; it names persistent mechanical block, stability, reconstructibility, and associated injury as considerations. It no longer presents `>2 mm OR >30%` as an automatic ORIF trigger. Mason III wording now says multifragmentary does not automatically mean irreconstructible and avoids prescribing arthroplasty solely from the class. The Mason IV treatment note preserves the dislocation-reduction sequence, then leaves fracture care to orthopaedic assessment of stability, reconstructibility, associated injuries, and patient factors. No Mason class automatically maps to a treatment in that section. The source-tied MCQ and cards test morphology, not an invented treatment rule.

Supplemental treatment context is intentionally limited. A systematic review of isolated Mason II fractures found low-level evidence and insufficient evidence for firm conclusions on optimal treatment ([Mulders et al., 2013, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22622090/)); a later systematic review similarly found no clear consensus for stable isolated Type II fractures without a mechanical block ([Lindenhovius et al., 2021, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33197586/)). A retrospective ORIF series compared simple and comminuted fractures and concluded that selected fractures with more than three fragments can be considered for fixation, which directly contradicts a categorical “ORIF impossible” statement ([Klug et al., 2022, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35545488/)). These studies do not establish a universal treatment algorithm; specialist scientific review remains pending.

The take-home thresholds now repeat the source's strict `<2 mm` and `>2 mm OR >30%` inequalities, state that exactly 2 mm without >30% articular involvement is unassigned by this PDF page, and explicitly reject a class-only treatment mapping. The Essex-Lopresti flashcard was also revised to avoid “must replace / never resect” absolutism: it emphasizes checking longitudinal stability and basing preservation, fixation, or replacement on reconstructibility and associated injuries. A small retrospective series found no missed Essex-Lopresti injury among selected acute resections with stability assessment and challenges a blanket excision dogma, but its 11-patient sample cannot set a general rule ([Kachooei et al., 2021, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34511427/)). A reported missed lesion case describes later proximal migration after delayed radial-head resection ([Middendorf et al., 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11751-013-0153-z)); this supports caution when longitudinal instability is present, not an automatic prosthesis for every case. The related terrible-triad card now also avoids mandating surgery for all components in every patient.
